The public data distribution explains why the demo result is weak evidence of generalization. TRAIN320 contains only 20 distinct first-two-background blocks, two query strings and four color-preference lists. Every public demo exactly reuses a TRAIN first-two-background block, seen view/cart product pattern and color list. Browse changes only the query. In contrast, none of the 32 development cases has a seen background block, view/cart pattern, query string or color list. Group separation alone therefore does not make the ten demo motifs new semantic tests.

A post-hoc read of all 32 public cases found the intended categories generally clear. The A backgrounds explicitly state a “main practical interest” and a secondary need: cacao needs a drinking cup, private audio needs headphones, drying after swimming needs a towel, and bright outdoor conditions suggest sunglasses. B publicly purchases the first item while retaining the explicit avoid-duplicates policy, leaving the secondary need. Mild ambiguity exists for overnight bag versus daypack and written pages versus a notebook, and assigning every other catalog product grade zero is harsher than real shopping usefulness. These authored priorities are not uniquely correct human purchases. Nevertheless, the weak grade-at-least-two and first-three results cannot be explained by merely swapping the two stated useful choices.

This review cannot identify the embedding, pooling, projection head or data as the sole cause. Perfect TRAIN with poor new-combination results establishes a transfer problem. The narrow background/query/color templates and correlated history patterns give the learned projection many shortcuts. Broader independently authored TRAIN wording, combinations of explicit needs, priorities, ownership, distracting views/carts and color phrasing are justified next experiments. More capacity or relaxed labels are not established fixes.
